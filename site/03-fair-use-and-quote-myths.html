Plain-English IP Guide for Small Creators

Fair Use and Quote Myths

"Fair use" is probably the most-quoted, least-understood phrase on the internet. Let's replace the folklore with what the doctrine actually says, in language a friend would use.

What fair use really is

Fair use is a legal defense, not a permission slip. That's the first big reframe: you don't qualify first and then publish. You publish, someone objects, and then you argue "no, this use is fair." That difference explains why the advice below sounds less certain than the internet wants it to be.

In the U.S., a judge weighs four factors:

  1. Purpose and character — Are you using it to say something new (commentary, criticism, parody, education) or mostly to ride along on someone else's work? Transformative uses (adding something with new meaning, message, or purpose) fare much better.
  2. Nature of the original — Using a factual book weighs toward fair use; using someone's creative artwork, song, or photo weighs against.
  3. Amount — Not just "how much," but how much matters. Taking 10% of a book can be too much if the 10% is the punchline; republishing an entire low-res photo can be fair if your point is the photo itself (criticizing it) and you couldn't meaningfully use less.
  4. Effect on the market — Would your use substitute for buying the original? A quote in a review usually doesn't. A chapter printed in your course reader usually does.

No factor wins alone, and the same four factors don't even apply everywhere — fair use is U.S. law. In the U.K. it's "fair dealing," which is narrower and listed by purpose (criticism, review, news reporting...). In Canada it's "fair dealing" plus a user-rights framework. Same instinct, different rules.

The myths, one by one

  • Myth: "Under 30 words / 5 notes / 10% is automatically fine." There is no magic number in U.S. law. A court looks at whether the snippet is the heart of the work, not the word count. On the flip side, short quotes can be fine — the myth is that any fixed number settles it.

  • Myth: "Attribution is a cure-all." Credit does not equal permission. Giving someone credit for their photo while you use it commercially without a license is still infringement — it just means you knew whose photo it was. (It can even be worse for you at trial.)

  • Myth: "It's on the internet, so it's public domain." Everything online is copyrighted by default. Public domain means the copyright expired or was given up — see Public domain and Creative Commons.

  • Myth: "I gave them a shoutout in exchange, so we're even." Unless there's a real license or an agreed exchange (and ideally something in writing), a shoutout is not consideration and not consent.

  • Myth: "It's fair use as long as I say it's fair use." Labeling your post "fair use" changes nothing. YouTube's Content ID isn't a judge, either; a dispute is not the same as a determination.

  • Myth: "Brief quotes from a song are fine like quoted book reviews." Song lyrics are the most-fought-over trap for authors. Lyric owners (publishers) license quotes aggressively and rarely treat short quotes as free. Practical playbook: quote at most a line or two that your argument actually needs, keep it clearly in the service of your commentary, or paraphrase instead. And never quote lyrics on a book cover or in ad copy.

  • Myth: "Fair use covers fan-covers, fan-art, and reading books on video." Fan projects live in gray zones that are shrinking for anything that substitutes an official product (a full audiobook reading, for example) or that monetizes borrowing. Different rules took over in real cases.

Where fair use is genuinely friendly to small creators

  • Quoting a paragraph or two of someone's book as part of your review of that book — classic fair use, when the quote serves the critique.
  • Screenshotting something to mock or explain it in your tutorial, reporting, or criticism, kept to the minimum that makes the point.
  • Parody that targets the original (Weird-Al-style ripostes), though parody of something else using someone's work is a different fight.

A safe default for your LLC

Before you use someone else's work because "this is probably fine":

  1. Ask "is my name on this merely quoted, or am I riding it?"
  2. If it's merely quoted and that quote is needed to make your point — say so, quote the minimum, name the creator, and note it in your permission log.
  3. If you can't say those two sentences honestly, license it, replace it, or drop it.
