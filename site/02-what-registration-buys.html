Plain-English IP Guide for Small Creators

What Registration Actually Buys

You already own copyright the moment you publish (see Copyright the moment you publish). So why would anyone fill out a government form and pay a fee? Because in the United States, a handful of genuinely useful rights are locked behind registration — and the fee is small enough that it's an honest hobby budget line.

Here's what the form buys, in the order it matters to a small creator.

1. The right to sue at all (in the U.S.)

Unregistered copyright exists but is mostly theoretical in America. To file a copyright infringement lawsuit over an American work, you must first register it with the U.S. Copyright Office. A court has to dismiss the case if the registration hasn't been filed yet. (Courts do have limited discretion to let you register mid-case, but starting with the form is how real authors do it.)

2. Statutory damages — the amount people actually fight over

This is the big one, and it's the reason people say "register early."

  • Register before infringement, or within 3 months of first publication: you may claim statutory damages (a per-work amount the judge can set between a few hundred dollars and up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement, without proving your actual losses) plus attorney's fees.
  • Register later: you're generally stuck with actual damages — profits the infringer made, real money you lost — which for a small creator is often a mountain of tiny, unprovable numbers and no lawyer will take the case.

Put simply: timely registration turns your copyright from a moral victory into a practical enforcement tool.

3. A public record that makes people think twice

A registration puts your work in a searchable federal database. That matters less for enforcement and more for scare avoidance: it gives you a clean, official-looking fact to send someone who's about to print your cover art on merch, and it gives lawyers a quick way to confirm you're the real owner.

4. A customs bonus (yes, really)

U.S. works registered with the Copyright Office can be recorded with U.S. Customs and Border Protection, which helps stop counterfeit imports of your book, print, or merch. If your LLC ever sells physical goods, this is a real one-page benefit most people never hear about.

What registration does NOT do

  • It does not create your copyright. That happened at the moment you wrote.
  • It does not protect ideas, titles, or names. Titles, short phrases, and character concepts get no copyright; that's a trademark conversation (see Trademark basics for series names).
  • It does not "prove" you wrote it. It's a presumption of ownership and a default court-ready record, but it's not magic; a false registration can hurt you badly.
  • It does not fix borrowed material. If your book includes someone else's art, registering the whole thing invites exactly the dispute you were trying to avoid — see Accidental reuse.

What it costs (as of writing this guide, U.S. only)

  • A single-author single-work registration is the cheapest tier, roughly the price of a paperback (tens of dollars, not hundreds).
  • Group registrations exist for databases of short posts, and for works published in a period (e.g., a newsletter archive), which is how many bloggers and LLCs register a year's output in one filing for one fee.

Check the official price before filing. Costs and rules change; the Copyright Office's site (copyright.gov) always shows the current fees and has plain-language walkthroughs of the online form.

When an LLC should register, in one paragraph

If your LLC publishes something with real commercial value — a book on KDP, a paid course, art you sell prints of — register it within three months of publishing it. That single habit captures the statutory-damages benefit for almost anything that can be infringed. For everyday blog posts and social content, registering everything is overkill; register the pieces you could realistically sell or license.
