Plain-English IP Guide for Small Creators

Copyright: You Have It the Moment You Publish

Here's the single most reassuring thing anyone will ever tell you about copyright, and it's true in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and most of the world: the moment you create something and fix it in a tangible form, you own a copyright in it.

Not the moment the Copyright Office approves something. Not the moment you add a © to it. The moment your fingers stop moving.

What "fixed in a tangible form" actually means

Fixed just means it exists somewhere you can point to:

  • A manuscript saved in your Documents folder. Copyrighted. (If your word processor auto-saves, you have a copyright every thirty seconds.)
  • A blog post published on your site. Copyrighted the moment it went live.
  • A video recorded on your phone. Copyrighted the moment you pressed record.
  • Cover art for your book. Copyrighted as soon as the file exists.

You do not have to publish it for copyright to exist — publishing isn't even part of it. You do not have to register it. You do not have to mail it to yourself. (The famous "poor man's copyright" — mailing yourself a copy - proves almost nothing and courts know it. Skip it.)

But an important detail cuts the other way too: ideas are not copyrighted. Your specific expression — the actual words, the actual drawing, the actual melody — belongs to you. The idea of "a nurse explains AI in plain English" belongs to nobody; the book you are writing about it belongs to you.

What you own, and what you don't

When Dylan publishes a chapter of The Massive AI Manual on dylanasdale.github.io, the copyright in that chapter is his and his LLC's from the moment it's visible on the page. What comes with it is a bundle of rights:

  1. Reproduce — make copies of it.
  2. Distribute — sell it, give it away, put it on KDP.
  3. Display or perform — post it publicly, read it aloud.
  4. Derivative works — the right to make sequels, translations, film adaptations, audiobooks.

Anybody else who does any of those things to your work without permission is infringing. You don't need a lawyer or a registration to be right about that.

What you never bought from the internet: the photos in a Google Images search, the font you downloaded, the song in a TikTok sound, the stock art your design app uses. Someone else's copyright doesn't disappear just because their file was easy to copy. That's the other half of this guide series.

What a © notice actually does

The little © line on a book's copyright page is a signal, not a condition. Your copyright exists without it. What a proper notice does:

  • tells scammers and lazy "opportunity" accountants where to go
  • removes a small set of "I honestly didn't know this was protected" excuses, although American law has mostly buried those excuses too

A practical notice for a self-published book looks like:

Copyright © 2026 Asdale Dynamics, LLC
All rights reserved.

That's it. There is no required format, no required wording, and no office to file it with.

The parts of "your" work you probably don't own

A reality check for authors and small LLCs, so nothing here surprises you:

  • KDP, Gumroad, Substack, etc. — the platforms own nothing of your content, but their terms of service DO matter: some take a license to distribute and display what you post. That license usually ends when you delete the work. Read the two paragraphs about "Your Content" once; you don't need to memorize the whole contract.
  • Fonts — the font file on your computer is someone else's copyrighted software. The text you type with it (the words in your book) is yours. See Licensing music and fonts for the part people get wrecked on: using fonts in images.
  • Stock photos and artwork — you never owned the copyright; you bought a license to use it. Keep the receipt in your permission log.
  • A work you paid a designer to make — congratulations, you may not own the copyright unless the contract says so in writing. More in Accidental reuse of other people's assets.

The one-sentence takeaway

You already have copyright the moment you publish. The whole rest of this guide is about (a) what you bother to claim on purpose, (b) what you accidentally took from someone else, and (c) what to do about both.
