Plain-English IP Guide for Small Creators

Glossary

Plain-English definitions of the words the legal pages throw around. These match how the guides use each term.

C

CC0 (Creative Commons Zero). A license where the creator gives up copyright entirely — the closest thing to public domain that a living person can offer. No credit needed. See Public domain and Creative Commons.

Commercial use. Anything that isn't purely private: selling, advertising, an LLC's blog, paid newsletters, Etsy. A "hobby-feeling" post from a business is commercial.

Copyright. The bundle of rights you get automatically when you fix an original work in tangible form. It does not protect ideas, titles, or names. See Copyright the moment you publish.

Creative Commons (CC). A family of standard licenses (CC0, BY, BY-SA, BY-NC, BY-ND) that creators attach to their works. All allow reuse without asking; the letters define the conditions.

D

Derivative work. A new work based on a previous one — a translation, a remix, a movie adaptation, a redrawn logo. Only the copyright owner may make (and license) derivatives.

DMCA takedown. The notice a platform sends when someone claims their copyrighted work appears somewhere it shouldn't. Getting one doesn't mean a lawsuit is coming; removing is almost always the right response.

F

Fair dealing. The narrower cousin of fair use in the U.K., Canada, and Australia — permitted purposes are specifically listed rather than weighed.

Fair use. The U.S. legal doctrine permitting some unlicensed uses of copyrighted work (criticism, commentary, parody, education), decided by court on four factors. It is a defense in a dispute, not a permission slip. See Fair use and quote myths.

Fixed in a tangible form. Saved to a file, printed, recorded, published — the trigger for copyright. Your draft chapter in a notes app qualifies.

I

Infringement. Using someone's protected work in a way their copyright or trademark doesn't allow. Attribution doesn't cure it.

International Class 16 / 41. The trademark classes covering printed materials (16) and education/publishing services (41) — the two that matter most for a book or course brand.

L

License. Permission to use a work in a defined way. A license is not ownership; read scope (commercial? platform? modification?).

M

Master rights / composition. The two copyrights in most music: the recording (master) and the underlying song (composition). Both often need separate licenses. See Licensing music and fonts.

P

Permission log. The running record of where your assets came from and what license or permission covers each — this repo ships a blank sheet: templates/permission-log.csv.

Public domain. A work with no remaining copyright owner. Use it freely, commercially, and without permission. See Public domain and Creative Commons.

R

Registration. Filing a work with the U.S. Copyright Office. It doesn't create copyright; it unlocks enforcement tools (statutory damages, the right to sue, customs). See What registration buys.

Royalty-free. A payment model (no per-use fees). Says nothing about whether the license grants you commercial-use rights.

S

Statutory damages. The pre-set per-work dollar range a U.S. court can award without proving actual losses — available only with timely copyright registration.

Sync license. The license to pair music with video (a trailer, a Reel).

T

Trademark. The legal protection of brand identifiers — names, logos — that customers use to tell products apart. Trademark rights in the U.S. grow from real use in commerce. See Trademark basics for series names.

™ (TM symbol). A claim that you treat something as your brand. Usable immediately, no registration required.

® (R symbol). May only be used after U.S. federal trademark registration.

W

Webfont license. The license tier for embedding a font file in a website. A desktop license does not cover this.

Education, never legal advice. Definitions here are plain-English guides to how the terms are used in these docs, not legal definitions. For real disputes, talk to a real IP attorney.
