# Public Domain and Creative Commons, Decoded

Two of the most useful tools in a small creator's toolbox — and two of the
most misread labels. This guide decodes both, specifically for **images and
book covers**.

## Public domain: the work belongs to everyone

A public-domain (PD) work has **no copyright owner anymore**. You can use it,
sell it, print it on shirts, edit it, publish it — no permission needed, no
credit required (though credit is kind and often expected by your audience).

**How work becomes public domain:**

- **Expired copyright.** In the U.S. as of January 2026, works published
  before **1931** are in the public domain, and every January 1 another year's
  worth ("1931 published in 2027") joins them. Use the current year minus 96
  as a rule of thumb, and verify before you build a business on it.
- **U.S. federal government works.** Photos, maps, charts, and documents made
  by U.S. federal employees as part of their jobs (NASA photos, Library of
  Congress images, USGS maps) are PD. Note: this does **not** include
  state/local government works, and it does **not** include works the
  *government bought* from contractors.
- **CC0 or explicit waivers.** Someone chose to give it up. See below.
- **Works the law never protected.** Facts, ideas, official texts, and most
  U.S. government edicts.

**Where a small creator can realistically find PD art:**

- Library of Congress (loc.gov) digital collections — well labeled.
- Smithsonian Open Access — millions of images, almost all CC0.
- Wikimedia Commons — filter by license; check each image's info box.
- The Met, Getty, and national galleries' open-access programs.

**PD trap #1: nothing to do with the underlying art is PD.** A 19th-century
painting is public domain; the *photograph of the painting* (a museum's
"faithful reproduction") is copyrighted in several countries, including the
U.K., even though the painting it copied is long expired. In the U.S., exact
reproductions of 2D PD art are generally not protected (Bridgeman v. Corel),
but U.K. sites often claim rights anyway — grab your scans from U.S.
institutions.

**PD trap #2: modern collections earn their own copyright.** A modern digital
scan of a map, with new color, a new title card, or editor commentary, can be
a new protected work even though the original is PD. Use the institution's
open-access versions when available.

**PD trap #3: "Found on Pinterest" is not PD.** See
[Accidental reuse](06-accidental-reuse.md).

## Creative Commons: a menu of pre-written permissions

All of them mean you may use the work without asking; the letters spell out
which rights the owner keeps. In one sentence each:

| License | The deal in one sentence |
| --- | --- |
| **CC0** | "Public-domain equivalent — use it for anything, no credit required." |
| **CC BY** | "Free to use, even commercially, if you credit the creator." |
| **CC BY-SA** | "Free to use and remix if you credit, and your remix keeps this license." |
| **CC BY-NC** | "Free to use if you credit — but **no commercial use**, no exceptions." |
| **CC BY-ND** | "Free to use as-is — but **no edited versions** of any kind." |

The **license family matters more than the letters**: the second word tells
you the deal. A quick decode habit:

- **BY** = give credit (always required in all four non-zero licenses).
- **SA** ("share-alike") = your follow-on work inherits the same license.
- **NC** ("noncommercial") = no selling, no ads, no business use. A small
  LLC's blog *is* commercial even when it feels hobbyish — don't use NC for
  anything your LLC posts.
- **ND** ("no derivatives") = don't crop, recolor, add text, or ship an
  edited version. That means no ND art inside a book cover you designed.

## Choosing CC for images and covers, in practice

1. **Search correctly.** Wikimedia Commons, Flickr (license filter), Openverse
   (official CC search) — each shows the license on the image page. Do not
   trust random aggregator sites; license text lives on the *source* page.
2. **Read the icons, not the vibes.** NC and ND are the two that bite
   creators, and they're stated in plain icons.
3. **Log it the day you use it.** Open your
   [permission log](templates/permission-log.csv) and add the image, the
   source URL, the exact license, and (for BY) the creator name you'll print
   in your credits.
4. **Credit properly but briefly.** Something like
   `“Example” by Jane Creator (CC BY 4.0)` is enough for most uses. For ND or
   SA, your edit *creates a derivative* — pick artwork you don't need to
   change, or choose a different license.
5. **When it's your own cover art:** you can publish it under CC BY to help
   your readers reuse it — or under full copyright if you plan to license it
   to merch. Selling prints and selling a "free to remix" cover can coexist,
   but mixing them invites confusion. Decide once and be consistent.

## One paragraph on copyrighted images you love anyway

A beautifully shot photo with no license badge is copyrighted by its owner,
full stop. Creating a book cover from it requires a real license (buy it, or
ask the owner and log the reply). "Credit to the photographer" is not a
license, and neither is a Google Image result. The good news: many creators
license affordably, respond to polite `permission` emails, and collections
like Unsplash/Pexels exist precisely for this use — with their own, freely
granted licenses that you should still log and read (their licensing terms
are specific, and they can change).
