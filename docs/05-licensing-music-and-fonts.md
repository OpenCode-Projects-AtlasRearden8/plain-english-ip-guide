# Licensing Music and Fonts for LLC Posts

Two asset types that small creators constantly touch — social-media music and
computer fonts — and both are actually *software licenses* in disguise. This
guide is the decode, with the traps that actually hurt LLCs.

## Fonts: the words are yours, the letters are licensed

The font *file* is copyrighted software. The *text you type* (your blog post,
your book, your captions) is yours. The trap lives in between: **what you're
licensed to do with the font file depends on the license tier.**

**The two licenses that matter for font files:**

- **Desktop license** — the one a font usually ships with. It covers using
  the letters in *static* outputs you make on your computer: your book's
  interior PDF, printed material, static images where the letters are
  flattened (a PNG cover, a quote graphic).
- **Webfont license** — covers the font *files being loaded by browsers* on
  your website. Desktop does not cover this; embedding the font file on your
  site without a webfont license is infringement even though you bought the
  desktop one.

**Fonts in images — the smallest gray area, and worth knowing:** rasterizing
text you typed into a static image (a PNG book cover) is generally accepted
inside the scope of a desktop license, *but* "generally accepted" is not a
promise; check the foundry's terms for anything that ships commercially or in
bulk. If your design tool embeds the font file itself (an interactive PDF,
a video annotation, a live webpage), you've crossed into an area that needs
the *right* license tier, full stop.

**Free font files — the fine print people skip:**

- **"Free for personal use" is not free for your LLC.** Any business use —
  including a hobby-feeling LLC blog, KDP covers, Etsy listings, paid
  newsletters — is commercial.
- **Free simulators like Google Fonts and Fontsource** are genuinely safe for
  commercial use (they use open licenses, mostly SIL Open Font License) —
  this is the safest default for almost everything you'll do. Same for
  open-source foundries like the League of Moveable Type.
- **"Free" template sites that embed commercial fonts** are the classic
  accidental-infringement source; a template being free does not transfer the
  font license inside it (see [Accidental reuse](06-accidental-reuse.md)).

**Practical rule:** for anything your LLC publishes, use Google Fonts unless
you have a specific reason, and put the font name + source + license into your
[permission log](templates/permission-log.csv) anyway. It takes ten seconds
and answers the question forever.

## Music: "royalty-free" is not "copyright-free"

Music rights are the only place in this guide with multiple *separate*
copyrights working simultaneously, which is why the music industry is the way
it is:

1. The **composition** (the song: melody, lyrics) — owned by writers and
   publishers.
2. The **recording** (the master) — owned by the recording side.
3. Sometimes a **sync license** (pairing music to video), a **mechanical
   license** (reproducing the composition), etc. — each is its own grant.

That's three to five rights-holders between you and a thirty-second clip. So
the decode for an LLC's posts is simple:

- **Never pull a song off YouTube, TikTok, a TV show, or a streaming service
  and put it in your own video, trailer, Reel, or ad.** That almost always
  needs licenses you don't have, from parties you've never spoken to.
- **"Royalty-free" means you don't pay per-use — it does not mean you
  obtained it properly.** A "royalty-free" track still needs its download
  page to *actually grant* you commercial-use rights.
- **Platform libraries are scope-limited.** YouTube's Audio Library and
  Instagram/Meta's sound library license music *for use on that platform in
  your posts*. Music that's fine in your platform-native Reel is often NOT
  licensed for a book trailer you upload to two platforms — and never for
  selling physical goods with the music in them.
- **Subscription libraries** (the paid "unlimited music" services) license
  you while you subscribe; what happens to videos *made* while subscribed
  varies by service — read the "what if I cancel" clause once.
- **"Copyright-free" usually means someone made it and released it under CC
  or CC0.** That's a real license — read it like any other
  ([CC decode](04-public-domain-and-creative-commons.md)), and remember NC
  clips are exactly the wrong pick for an LLC.

**Practical defaults:**

- Platform-native Reels, Shorts, TikToks: use that platform's own library, and
  log it.
- Book trailers and ads you'll post in several places: music from a service
  that explicitly grants commercial + multi-platform rights, and log it.
- Anything you'd sell (audiobooks, paid courses): really, truly,
  individually listed output from a licensed library, and log the *receipt*.

## Log the license where you can re-find it

The permission log exists because the question "wait, am I allowed to use
this again next quarter?" is otherwise unanswerable. Columns in
[templates/permission-log.csv](templates/permission-log.csv) cover exactly
this case — asset, source, license name, proof link, expiry.
