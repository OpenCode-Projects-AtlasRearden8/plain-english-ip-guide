# Accidentally Reusing Other People's Assets

Almost every IP problem a small creator runs into starts the same way: with a
file that was *right there*, easy to download, workable in Canva, and nobody
said "whose is this?" This guide lists the traps in order of how often they
hit small creators and LLCs — then the honest playbook for what to do when
you find one.

## The traps, most-common first

1. **Images from a Google/Pinterest/WeHeartIt search.** Every image online is
   copyrighted by default (see
   [Copyright the moment you publish](01-copyright-the-moment-you-publish.md)).
   Pinterest re-hosts other people's photos and strips the license
   information off them; searching there is like buying a car without asking
   who owns it.

2. **"Free" templates that embed paid fonts and stock photos.** The template
   is free; the assets inside it usually aren't. Opening it in your design
   app and exporting your own version ships the unlicensed parts right along
   with it.

3. **Product screenshots and app logos.** Screenshots of software, logos, and
   UI used in your *review or comparison* are usually covered by trademark
   fair-use-style "nominative use" plus honest description — but using a
   company's logo as your blog-post thumbnail, in an ad, or in your app's
   "trusted by" row is a different, and much worse, use. Rule of thumb:
   describing their product = fine. Borrowing their brand to dress up yours
   = not fine without permission.

4. **Gift and "free download" art from communities.** Art someone made and
   shared for free in a Discord or subreddit is copyrighted by that artist
   unless they attached a license. "Free to download" is not "free to
   publish in your book."

5. **Canva and other design-tool templates you sell or license onward.**
   Design tools allow their stock in finished designs, but *the licenses
   usually don't permit* you to sell the design standalone (a print-on-demand
   poster reproduced from pure stock, an asset pack you export for others).
   Read the two "what you can't do" bullets before selling anything.

6. **AI-generated assets with unclear upstream rights.** This space moves
   fast and the law is genuinely unsettled; treat a tool's terms as your one
   real contract. Practical cautions: don't generate known characters or
   logos by name, keep records of your prompts and outputs, and never assume
   "AI generated it" means no one can claim a right in it.

7. **Your freelancer's delivery.** If you paid a designer for a cover or a
   logo without a written transfer or license, **you may not own the
   copyright** in your own cover — under U.S. law, hiring someone (without
   the exact "work for hire" magic words in a signed writing) usually leaves
   the copyright with the *artist* and gives you only an implied license.
   This is the most fixable one: send them a two-line written agreement
   now, while everyone is friendly.

8. **Logos and brand marks in your own branding.** Borrowed "temporary" logos
   have a way of aging into shipped products. A series name or logo that
   eventually becomes your brand is trademark territory (see
   [Trademark basics for series names](07-trademark-basics-for-series-names.md)).

## What to do when you find one (the honest playbook)

Work down the list in order; stop when the problem is actually solved:

1. **Remove or replace.** The cheapest fix is always the same: take the asset
   out, swap in something you licensed, or drop it. Emotional sunk cost is
   not a reason to keep risk.
2. **License it after the fact.** For stock-style assets, buying the license
   retroactively really does close the gap for continuing uses — log the new
   receipt in your [permission log](templates/permission-log.csv).
3. **Ask for permission.** A short, polite email to the creator — what you
   used, where, why, and what happens if they say no — gets surprisingly far
   with individual artists. **Before asking, remove it**; then re-add it
   *only* if they say yes and you have it in writing. Never make permission a
   surprise after the fact.
4. **Tell the platform first, not the lawyer.** If a takedown arrives, the
   response is usually "remove, apologize, don't relitigate" — not denial.
   Contesting an infringement claim you know was fair is how small problems
   become big ones.
5. **Record it.** Whatever you did — removed, licensed, or got permission —
   one row in the log. Six months later you won't remember the email thread;
   the log will.

## The habit that prevents almost all of this

Before using any asset you didn't make, answer the **three-source question**:

- Where did this file come from? (The *page*, not the folder.)
- What does the license on that page actually say?
- Can I show all of this in one line of my permission log?

If any answer is missing, treat the asset as borrowed and either license it,
find an alternative you can document, or ask. Ten seconds now beats a takedown
later.
