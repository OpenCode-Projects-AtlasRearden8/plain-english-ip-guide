# Trademark Basics for Series Names

Copyrights are for *works* — books, art, songs (see
[Copyright the moment you publish](01-copyright-the-moment-you-publish.md)).
**Trademarks are for *brands* — the names, logos, and marks your readers use
to recognize you** in the marketplace. If your LLC is going to publish
regularly, the series name is where trademarks stop being optional trivia.

## Copyright ≠ trademark (the single most important decode)

- Copyright protects the *content you make* (the words on your pages, the
  cover art). It exists the moment you publish.
- Trademark protects *your identity in commerce* — the thing customers look
  for: the brand name, the logo, sometimes the trade dress.
- Titles get **neither** by default: a single book's title gets no copyright
  (copyright never covers titles or short phrases) and the USPTO generally
  won't register a title of a single creative work. **A series name, though,
  is different — it identifies the *product line***, and product lines are
  exactly what trademarks protect best.

That's the whole reason "The Massive AI Manual" is fine as one title, while
"Plain-English Guides" *as a series brand* is trademark country.

## What a series trademark actually gets you

- **Peace of mind as you publish.** Nobody tells an LLC "you're fine" until
  they're attached to money. A series that will eventually become a business
  (courses, branded merch, paid newsletter) deserves one brainstorm of
  *naming diligence*.
- **The power to stop confusable newcomers.** Registration gives a legal
  presumption of ownership in the U.S. for the goods/classes listed (books =
  International Class 16; online courses = Class 41, roughly), and lets you
  use the ® after it registers.
- **Disaster insurance for the version of you with deadlines.** Small
  creators are uniquely likely to be hurt, not helped, by discovering a
  conflict after three published books and a merch store.

## Knockout searches you can do in one evening

You are not trying to *prove* anything; you're trying to find reasons to
rename *before* printing. Do all of these — in order — using the *exact*
spelling you'd use on a cover:

1. **Amazon + Google search** for the exact phrase, ideally with
   `"quoted phrase"` and your product category word (`books`, `KDP`).
2. **USPTO TESS / Trademark Search** — search the name in **live
   trademarks**, in Classes **16** (printed materials) and **41** (education
   and publishing), plus any class matching your real plans.
3. **KDP** — search books by that exact series title to rule out direct
   competition (there can still be recognized conflicts even without
   registration).
4. **Domain and social handles** — not legal requirements, but a brand you
   can't own a coherent URL for is a pain.
5. **OG image search** — the cover art and logo check that catches fan
   borrowings older than your search began.

Red flag rules while searching:

- **Same or similar name, same or similar products:** rename. This is the
  case that matters, and it's judge-and-common-sense (likelihood of
  confusion), not an exact-match game.
- **Different field, famous name:** still rename. "Famous marks" get
  protection far beyond their product categories.
- **Different field, ordinary name:** generally fine — that's why dozens of
  businesses share the word "Summit."

## ™ vs ® — the everyday symbols

- **™:** you can use this *today*, for free, on anything you'd honestly call
  your brand. It claims nothing except "I treat this as my brand" — no
  registration required, and it's a fair warning shot at strangers. Small
  creators forget they're allowed to use it.
- **®:** federal registration in the U.S. **only after** the USPTO grants it.
  Using ® without registration is federal fraud territory — in *both*
  directions, and it can make you look worse in every later dispute.
  Before it's registered: use ™ or nothing.

## Registering — the skim version (U.S.)

1. Confirm the knockout searches came back clean.
2. File at USPTO.gov in the classes that match your real activities.
3. Declare a **specimen** (proof you use it in commerce, e.g., a published
   book listing).
4. Respond to any examiner questions (this happens in most small filings).
5. Use it consistently in public from that day forward: same spelling, same
   goods, stated as a brand.

Small-creator reality check: **you can afford to wait until a series is
real.** You can't afford to *rename after it's real* — so do the knockout
searches on day one, use ™ from then on, and register when the series has
earnings behind it.

## The two-line plan

- Names: knockout search first, ™ from day one, ® when you register.
- Works: copyright is already yours from the moment you published. (Nothing
  to do but keep the receipts — start the
  [permission log](templates/permission-log.csv).)

> **Education, never legal advice.** This guide is information, not counsel.
> It does not replace a real trademark attorney, and nothing in it creates an
> attorney–client relationship. Registration procedures, fees, and class
> details change — always check USPTO.gov before filing anything.
